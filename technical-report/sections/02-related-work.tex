\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

In this section, we give an overview of related work on tool support for \SLRs.
We first look at the methodology and at studies about \SLR tools.
Then, we discuss existing tools and, lastly, the use of \LLMs in \SLRs.

\subsection{Methodology and Studies on Tool Support}\label{sec:related-work-studies}

% Methodology
The \SLR method in \SE goes back to the guidelines by Kitchenham and Charters~\cite{kitchenham2007guidelines} and the book by Kitchenham et al.~\cite{kitchenham2015evidence}.
Petersen et al.~\cite{petersen2015guidelines} describe systematic mapping studies, and Wohlin~\cite{wohlin2014guidelines} describes snowballing as a search strategy.
\PRISMA 2020~\cite{page2021prisma} defines what a systematic review reports, including a flow diagram of found, screened, and included studies.
These guidelines define the activities that \SaiLoR supports.
\SaiLoR does not add new methodology.
Instead, it records the data that these guidelines ask for.

% Studies about tools
Several studies look at tools for \SLRs.
Marshall and Brereton~\cite{marshall2013tools} map the tools that \SE researchers proposed, and Marshall et al.~\cite{marshall2014tools} compare the features of \SLR tools in \SE.
Hassler et al.~\cite{hassler2014outcomes} collected barriers to the \SLR process in a community workshop.
Al-Zubidy and Carver~\cite{alzubidy2019identification} derived requirements for tools that support the search activity.
Felizardo and Carver~\cite{felizardo2020automating} give an overview of how \SLR activities can be automated, and van Dinter et al.~\cite{vandinter2021automation} review automation approaches in an \SLR of their own.
Most of the studies that van Dinter et al. found automate the selection of primary studies.
Outside \SE, Harrison et al.~\cite{harrison2020software} evaluate tools for title and abstract screening in healthcare.
These studies show that tool support exists for many activities.
Our work differs in its goal.
We do not survey or compare tools but describe the design of one tool and how we keep it traceable.

\subsection{Tools for Systematic Literature Reviews}\label{sec:related-work-tools}

% Hosted tools
A large group of tools are hosted web applications.
Covidence~\cite{covidence} is a web platform that covers screening, full-text review, risk of bias, and data extraction.
Rayyan~\cite{ouzzani2016rayyan} is a web and mobile app that supports screening with semi-automated suggestions.
CADIMA~\cite{kohl2018cadima} is an open-access online tool by the Julius K\"uhn-Institut for the conduct and reporting of systematic reviews and maps.
Parsifal~\cite{parsifal} is an online platform for planning, conducting, and reporting \SLRs in \SE with support for collaboration.
In all four tools, the review data lives on a server, and collaboration happens through the shared online project.
\SaiLoR takes the opposite route.
The review data stays in local files, and reviewers exchange it through git, a hosting service of their choice, or not at all.
Therefore, reviewers can inspect, archive, and publish the raw data with standard tools.

% Tools from SE research
Some tools come from \SE research.
StArt~\cite{hernandes2012start,fabbri2016start} supports the whole systematic review process, and Hernandes et al.~\cite{hernandes2012start} evaluated it with GQM and TAM.
SLR-Tool~\cite{fernandezsaez2010slrtool} is a free tool that follows the process by Kitchenham and adds text mining and export to BibTeX and RIS.
Both tools target the same community as \SaiLoR.
The publications on these tools do not cover independent extraction by several reviewers with a consolidation step, and they do not cover collaboration through version control.

% Screening-focused tools
ASReview~\cite{vandeschoot2021asreview} is an open-source framework that uses active learning to rank the records that a reviewer should screen next.
Like \SaiLoR, ASReview can run locally.
However, ASReview focuses on screening, while \SaiLoR focuses on extraction and consolidation and offers screening as a preceding step.
\SaiLoR does not rank records with active learning.
The two tools can complement each other, because \SaiLoR imports BibTeX, RIS, and CSL-JSON files.

\subsection{Large Language Models in Systematic Literature Reviews}\label{sec:related-work-llm}

% LLM studies
Recently, many studies evaluate \LLMs for single \SLR activities.
For selection, Felizardo et al.~\cite{felizardo2024chatgpt} evaluate the accuracy of ChatGPT on \SLRs in \SE, and Syriani et al.~\cite{syriani2024screening} evaluate ChatGPT for screening on several datasets.
% TODO verify: the conclusion of Felizardo et al. that SE researchers should not outsource the selection activity to ChatGPT.
Felizardo et al. advise against handing the selection over to ChatGPT.
For extraction, Felizardo et al.~\cite{felizardo2024dataextraction} and Gartlehner et al.~\cite{gartlehner2024data} report proof-of-concept studies.
% TODO verify: summary of the Khraisha et al. results.
Khraisha et al.~\cite{khraisha2024llm} test GPT-4 for screening and extraction across languages and find mixed results.
Other work uses ChatGPT to formulate Boolean search queries~\cite{wang2023chatgpt} or across several stages of a review~\cite{alshami2023harnessing}.
These studies evaluate the models.
They do not address how a tool should present model output so that the reviewer stays in control and the use of the model stays visible in the report.
\SaiLoR addresses this second question.
Every proposal is a suggestion that a reviewer must apply, the model can act as an additional reviewer whose answers go through the same consolidation, and the project records each use of the model.

% Gap
Overall, existing tools either keep the review data on a server or focus on a single activity.
To the best of our knowledge, no tool yet combines local, versioned review data, schema-driven extraction with evidence links, consolidation of independent reviewers, and controlled \LLM assistance.
